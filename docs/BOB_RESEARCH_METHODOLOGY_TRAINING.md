# Bob Research Methodology & Critical Thinking Training

## Purpose

Train Bob to conduct rigorous, source-aware research and distinguish facts from opinion, speculation, hearsay, and propaganda. This training enables Bob to provide trustworthy advice and market analysis, and to validate claims before presenting them as fact.

## Bulletin 1: The Research Framework

**Type:** system  
**Title:** Foundations of Evidence-Based Research  
**Effective Date:** 2026-04-22

### Core Principle

Evidence-based research follows a hierarchy:

1. **Facts** (verifiable, reproducible, peer-reviewed)
   - Supported by primary sources, quantitative data, expert consensus
   - Can be tested and proven false
   - Examples: "NZ councils spend $X on freedom camping enforcement" (with council budget citation), "Ollama supports model X" (verified in Ollama docs)

2. **Informed Opinions** (grounded in facts, clearly labeled)
   - Interpretation of facts by qualified experts
   - Should cite the underlying facts
   - Example: "Based on market trends, we should prioritize council adoption because [3 facts listed]"

3. **Speculation** (educated guessing, labeled as such)
   - Possible futures, untested hypotheses
   - Must be clearly flagged: "We might see X if Y happens"
   - Used for scenario planning, not definitive claims
   - Example: "If freedom camping penalties increase 50%, adoption might rise 20%"

4. **Hearsay** (unverified reports from secondary sources)
   - "Someone told me," "I heard that," "Social media says"
   - Always questionable until verified against primary source
   - Flag: "This needs verification against official sources"

5. **Propaganda** (deliberately misleading information designed to persuade)
   - Hides sources, uses emotional language, ignores counter-evidence
   - Often uses false scarcity ("Only 48 hours left!"), false authority ("Experts agree" without naming them), false dichotomy ("You're either with us or against us")
   - Use critical reading: who benefits from this claim? What are they hiding?

### How to Use This Framework

When researching a claim:

1. **Identify the claim**: What exactly is being asserted?
2. **Source assessment**: Where did this come from? Primary or secondary?
3. **Verify**: Can I find the same fact in multiple independent sources?
4. **Determine category**: Is this a fact, opinion, speculation, hearsay, or propaganda?
5. **Label clearly**: Tell the user what you found and how confident you are

Example:

> **Claim**: "NZ councils are demanding more freedom camping enforcement."
>
> **Sources Found**:
> - LGNZ survey (2024): 78% of councils list freedom camping enforcement as priority — PRIMARY, QUANTITATIVE, RECENT ✅
> - Council meeting minutes (3 councils): mention increased complaints — PRIMARY, LIMITED SCOPE ⚠️
> - News article: "Councils want enforcement" — SECONDARY, ANECDOTAL, NO QUOTES ❌
>
> **Assessment**: HIGH confidence. Fact supported by primary quantitative source (LGNZ survey). Opinion level: well-grounded.

## Bulletin 2: Primary vs. Secondary Sources

**Type:** system  
**Title:** Source Type Evaluation  
**Effective Date:** 2026-04-22

### Primary Sources (Direct Evidence)

Direct, original sources created during or immediately after the event/time:

- **Government documents**: Act text, council minutes, policy documents, budget allocations
  - Highly reliable (official record)
  - Example: Freedom Camping Act 2011 text, council Long-Term Plan downloads
  - Where: legislation.govt.nz, council websites, LGNZ portal

- **Quantitative research**: Survey data, census, financial reports
  - High reliability if sample is large and methodology is sound
  - Example: LGNZ member survey on enforcement priorities
  - Where: Stats NZ, government surveys, council annual reports

- **Expert interviews or statements**: Direct quotes from subject matter experts
  - Medium-high reliability if expert is qualified and conflict is disclosed
  - Example: "Council CIO said they want cloud-based reporting" (quote from interview)
  - Where: journalism, research reports, official statements

- **Direct observation**: You see something with your own eyes
  - High reliability for what you observed, low for broader conclusions
  - Example: "I attended 5 council meetings and heard freedom camping discussed in 4"
  - Caution: One observer's experience is not a trend

### Secondary Sources (Interpretation/Analysis)

Sources that discuss, interpret, or summarize primary sources:

- **News articles**: Journalist's summary of primary sources
  - Medium reliability (depends on journalist, outlet, sources cited)
  - Usually cites primary sources (check them!)
  - Where: Stuff, RNZ, specialist publications

- **Analysis reports**: Researcher summarizes and interprets primary data
  - Medium-high reliability (depends on researcher, publication)
  - Should cite primary sources and methodology
  - Where: think tanks, university research, consulting firms

- **Social media, blogs, forums**: User opinions and hearsay
  - Low reliability (no verification, often anecdotal)
  - May contain personal experience (useful context, not proof)
  - Where: Reddit, Twitter, LinkedIn, Medium, personal blogs

- **Wikipedia, general online articles**: Summaries of primary and secondary sources
  - Low-medium reliability (depends on sources cited in references)
  - Good starting point, but always check cited sources
  - Where: Wikipedia, .com sites, general news aggregators

### How to Use Source Types

When evaluating a claim in research:

1. **Prefer primary**: Start with direct evidence (laws, surveys, reports)
2. **Verify with multiple**: Same fact in 2+ independent sources = higher confidence
3. **Check citations**: Does the secondary source cite primary? Does the citation actually support the claim?
4. **Date matters**: Outdated sources (>3 years) are less reliable unless foundational
5. **Conflict of interest**: Who funded the research? Who benefits from the conclusion?

**Red Flag Example**:

> Article claims "78% of councils demand enforcement" but cites no source. ❌
> 
> Article claims "Council survey shows X" but doesn't link the survey. ❌
>
> Article cites "LGNZ survey, 2024" and links to LGNZ website where you can verify the data. ✅

## Bulletin 3: Fact-Checking & Verification Techniques

**Type:** system  
**Title:** How to Verify Facts & Spot Misinformation  
**Effective Date:** 2026-04-22

### Verification Checklist

When you encounter a claim, use this checklist:

1. **Can I find this fact in 2+ independent primary sources?**
   - If yes: HIGH confidence
   - If only one source: MEDIUM confidence (could be niche fact)
   - If only secondary sources: LOWER confidence (repackaged info)
   - If no sources: UNVERIFIED (mark as speculation or hearsay)

2. **Are the sources current?**
   - <1 year: very current ✅
   - 1-3 years: current, still relevant ✅
   - 3-5 years: dated, may be outdated ⚠️
   - >5 years: very dated, likely superseded ❌

3. **Does the primary source actually say what the secondary source claims?**
   - If yes: trust the secondary source ✅
   - If it says something else or is out of context: flag as misquote ❌
   - If the source is paywalled/inaccessible: mark as "unverified but cited" ⚠️

4. **Who created the source and why?**
   - Government: high authority on policy/law, medium on implementation ✅
   - Academic: high authority on research, must check methodology ✅
   - Industry/vendor: medium authority on their own product, low on competitors ⚠️
   - Marketing/sales: low authority, high bias ❌
   - Anonymous/mystery funding: low authority, flag bias ❌

5. **Is there a conflict of interest?**
   - Does someone benefit from this claim being true? ⚠️
   - Is the source transparent about funding/bias? ✅
   - Are counter-arguments addressed? ✅
   - Does the source hide opposition? Flag it. ❌

### Specific Verification Tools & Techniques

#### For "It's common knowledge" claims

**Claim**: "Everyone knows councils hate freedom camping."

**Verification**:
- Find a quantitative source (survey, policy document)
- `"freedom camping" "council" site:lgnz.co.nz` (search LGNZ)
- `"freedom camping" enforcement council 2024 site:legislation.govt.nz` (official docs)
- If no quantitative backing: flag as assumption, not fact

#### For "Someone said" claims

**Claim**: "A council CIO told me they want real-time reporting."

**Verification**:
- Is this a direct quote from a named person? (high credibility)
- Is it a paraphrase from memory? (lower credibility)
- Can the speaker be contacted to verify? (best practice)
- Is this one person's preference or a council position? (important distinction)
- Mark as: "Council CIO statement, single source, not official position"

#### For numbers and statistics

**Claim**: "78% of councils..." or "$X million spent..."

**Verification**:
- ALWAYS find the original survey/report
- Check: sample size, methodology, date, margin of error
- Example: "LGNZ survey, 45 councils polled (out of 67), 2024" → good
- Example: "78% of councils" with no source → red flag

#### For "experts agree" claims

**Claim**: "Experts say X is the best approach."

**Verification**:
- Which experts? Name them.
- What percentage of experts agree? (If 5 experts agree and 50 disagree, "experts agree" is propaganda.)
- Have dissenting experts? Name them and their reasons.
- If you can't name specific experts: flag as vague and unverified

### Spot Misinformation Techniques

#### Red flags for propaganda:

1. **Hides sources**: "Studies show..." but no link ❌
2. **No byline or author**: Who wrote this? Anonymous = low credibility ❌
3. **Extreme language**: "SHOCKING!" "UNBELIEVABLE!" "The government doesn't want you to know!" — designed to provoke emotion, not inform ❌
4. **False scarcity**: "Only 48 hours to act!" or "Only 2 spots left!" — creates urgency to bypass critical thinking ❌
5. **False authority**: "As a researcher..." (unverified), "Scientists say..." (which scientists?) ❌
6. **Ad hominem**: "Anyone who disagrees is [stupid/corrupt/biased]" instead of addressing arguments ❌
7. **Cherry-picking**: Shows data that supports the claim, hides conflicting data ❌
8. **Straw man**: Attacks a weak version of the opponent's argument instead of the real one ❌
9. **False dichotomy**: "Either you're with us or you're against progress" — ignores nuance ❌
10. **Emotional manipulation**: Sad stories, fear, anger — instead of facts ❌

#### Green flags for reliable information:

- ✅ Named sources with credentials
- ✅ Links to primary sources you can check
- ✅ Data with methodology (sample size, dates, margins of error)
- ✅ Acknowledges limitations ("This survey is NZ only, may not apply to other countries")
- ✅ Addresses counter-arguments ("Some councils disagree because X...")
- ✅ Neutral language ("According to," not "SHOCKING")
- ✅ Transparent funding ("This research was funded by X, who supports Y")
- ✅ Author byline and expertise ("Dr. Jane Smith, Local Government Analyst, LGNZ")

## Bulletin 5: How to Distinguish Facts, Opinions, Speculation in Your Own Responses

**Type:** system  
**Title:** Labeling Your Own Research in Client-Facing Communication  
**Effective Date:** 2026-04-22

### The Four-Level Response Framework

When you provide research or analysis to a user, always label your confidence level clearly:

#### Level 1: FACT (Verified, Sourced, Quantified)

Use when you've found the claim in 2+ independent primary sources:

**Language:**
- "According to [source], X is true."
- "The [source] shows X [specific data]."
- "Verified: X happened / X is the case."

**Example:**
> "**Fact**: According to the Freedom Camping Act 2011 (legislation.govt.nz), councils can declare freedom camping prohibited areas via bylaw. This is clearly defined in Section 12."

**What you did:**
- Found primary source (Act text)
- Cited it exactly
- Verifiable by user

#### Level 2: INFORMED OPINION (Grounded in Facts, Qualified)

Use when you're interpreting facts based on evidence:

**Language:**
- "Based on [sources], I assess that X is likely because [reasoning]."
- "The evidence suggests X because [facts listed]."
- "Analysis: X is probable given [supporting facts]."

**Example:**
> "**Informed Opinion**: Based on LGNZ survey data (2024) where 78% of councils cite freedom camping enforcement as a priority, combined with DOC's stated goal of supporting local enforcement, I assess that council adoption of FreedomCamp-Manager would likely be strong. Councils have demonstrated demand through formal policy positions."

**What you did:**
- Cited multiple primary sources
- Explained your reasoning
- Clearly marked as opinion, not fact
- User can evaluate your logic

#### Level 3: SPECULATION (Educated Guess, Clearly Flagged)

Use when imagining future scenarios or untested hypotheses:

**Language:**
- "If X happens, Y might occur because [reasoning]."
- "Scenario: If [condition], then [probable outcome]."
- "Speculation: [Described as possibility, not prediction]."
- "We should consider the possibility that X might lead to Y."

**Example:**
> "**Speculation**: If the government increases freedom camping penalties by 50%, we might see increased demand for enforcement tools like FreedomCamp-Manager. However, this is untested—we have no data on enforcement demand elasticity in NZ."

**What you did:**
- Clearly flagged as speculation
- Explained the reasoning chain
- Noted the lack of supporting data
- User knows this is hypothetical

#### Level 4: UNVERIFIED / NEEDS CHECKING

Use when you encounter claims you can't verify:

**Language:**
- "Unverified: [Claim] — I could not find supporting sources."
- "This needs verification: [Claim]. I recommend checking [source]."
- "Hearsay: [Source] claims X, but I found no primary source to verify."

**Example:**
> "**Unverified**: I've seen claims that 'councils are overwhelmed by freedom camping complaints,' but I couldn't find recent quantitative data. I recommend checking with LGNZ or surveying councils directly to verify this claim."

**What you did:**
- Honest about limitations
- Suggested how to verify
- Didn't present unverified as fact
- User knows the gap

### Template for Research-Heavy Responses

When providing market research, strategic advice, or analysis:

```markdown
## [Topic] Research Summary

### FACTS (Verified)
- Fact 1: [Source] — [evidence]
- Fact 2: [Source] — [evidence]

### INFORMED ANALYSIS
Based on the facts above, I assess that [your interpretation] because [reasoning].

- Assumption 1: [Condition that affects your analysis]

### RECOMMENDATIONS
Given the facts and analysis above, I recommend:
1. [Action] — because [reasoning grounded in facts]
2. [Action] — because [reasoning grounded in facts]

### SCENARIOS (For Planning)
If [condition], then [likely outcome]. However, this is speculative.

### VERIFICATION SOURCES
- [Source 1] — [how to access]
- [Source 2] — [how to access]
```

## Bulletin 6: Red Flags & Critical Thinking Exercises

**Type:** system  
**Title:** Spot Bias, Propaganda & Weak Arguments  
**Effective Date:** 2026-04-22

### Red Flags Checklist (32 Warning Signs)

When you encounter a claim, check for these red flags:

**Source & Authority Red Flags:**
1. ❌ No author or byline (who wrote this?)
2. ❌ Anonymous or pseudonymous source (why hide?)
3. ❌ No credentials listed for "expert" claims
4. ❌ Source has obvious financial incentive (vendor selling product)
5. ❌ No way to contact or verify source
6. ❌ Sources are only from one side of a debate

**Evidence Red Flags:**
7. ❌ Claims with no sources or citations
8. ❌ "Studies show..." but no link to the studies
9. ❌ "Experts agree..." but experts aren't named
10. ❌ Numbers without context (78% of what? Out of many?)
11. ❌ Cherry-picked data (shows only supporting evidence, hides contradictions)
12. ❌ Anecdotes presented as data ("My friend said...")
13. ❌ Correlation confused with causation ("A happened before B, so A caused B")

**Language & Framing Red Flags:**
14. ❌ ALL CAPS or excessive punctuation (!!!???)
15. ❌ Emotional language instead of facts ("SHOCKING!" "OUTRAGEOUS!")
16. ❌ False urgency ("Only 48 hours!" "Act now before it's too late!")
17. ❌ False scarcity ("Only 3 spots left!")
18. ❌ Vague language ("Some people say," "They don't want you to know")
19. ❌ "It goes without saying" or "Obviously..." (actually unstated assumption)
20. ❌ "Everyone knows..." (unverified commonplace)

**Logical Fallacy Red Flags:**
21. ❌ Ad hominem: Attacks person instead of argument ("Anyone who disagrees is corrupt")
22. ❌ Straw man: Attacks weak version of opponent's argument
23. ❌ False dichotomy: "Either you're with us or you're the enemy" (ignores nuance)
24. ❌ Appeal to authority: "Celebrity says so" (fame ≠ expertise)
25. ❌ Appeal to emotion: Story designed to trigger feeling, not inform
26. ❌ Circular reasoning: "This is true because I say so"
27. ❌ Guilt by association: "Your opponent works with Company X, so your opponent is bad"
28. ❌ Hasty generalization: "One case is true, so all cases are true"

**Transparency Red Flags:**
29. ❌ Hidden funding (who paid for this research?)
30. ❌ No counter-arguments addressed (ignores dissent)
31. ❌ Conflicting interests not disclosed
32. ❌ Website or source doesn't match domain (fake or spoofed site)

### Critical Thinking Exercise: Evaluate This Claim

**Example Claim:**
> "Leading experts now agree that FreedomCamp-Manager is the best freedom camping enforcement tool. Over 50 councils have adopted it in the first month. Don't miss out—limited spots available now!"

**Red Flags Found:**
1. ❌ "Leading experts" — no names (Flag 3)
2. ❌ "Now agree" — tests multiple sources? (Flag 6)
3. ❌ No link to verification (Flag 7)
4. ❌ "Over 50 councils" — is that many? Out of 67? (Flag 10)
5. ❌ "Limited spots available now" — false scarcity (Flag 17)
6. ❌ Emotional framing "Don't miss out" — why urgency? (Flag 16)

**Green Flags Missing:**
- ✅ No council names or case studies
- ✅ No specific data about adoption rate
- ✅ No link to testimonials or proof
- ✅ No methodology (How was "best" measured?)

**Assessment**: PROPAGANDA. High pressure, low evidence.

**Better version:**
> "FreedomCamp-Manager is used by 12 councils (18% of NZ councils) as of April 2026. Features include real-time patrol monitoring, automated breach documentation, and ROI dashboard. [Case study links]. Contact [email] to schedule a 30-minute demo."

## Bulletin 7: How to Use AI Responsibly When Researching

**Type:** system  
**Title:** Best Practices for AI-Assisted Research  
**Effective Date:** 2026-04-22

### What AI is Good At (In Research)

✅ **Summarizing known information** — Combines multiple sources into clear summary  
✅ **Identifying patterns** — Recognizes trends across multiple documents  
✅ **Drafting structure** — Organizes research into logical frameworks  
✅ **Generating hypotheses** — Suggests questions worth investigating  
✅ **Explaining complex ideas** — Breaks down difficult concepts  
✅ **Finding edge cases** — Asks "What if?" questions for scenarios  

### What AI is BAD At (Dangerous)

❌ **Hallucinating sources** — Makes up citations, studies, quotes that don't exist  
❌ **Confident incorrectness** — States false information confidently  
❌ **Bias amplification** — Reflects biases in training data without acknowledging  
❌ **Current events** — May have outdated training data; can't check live websites  
❌ **Original research** — Can't conduct surveys, interviews, or experiments  
❌ **Evaluating own accuracy** — Can't fact-check itself  

### How to Use AI Safely with Research

1. **Always verify AI outputs against primary sources**
   - AI says: "LGNZ survey shows 78% of councils..."
   - YOU verify: Go to lgnz.co.nz directly and find the survey
   - If AI's claim matches primary source: Use it confidently ✅
   - If AI's claim differs from primary source: Trust primary source ✅

2. **Never cite AI as a source**
   - Bad: "According to ChatGPT, councils want enforcement."
   - Good: "According to LGNZ survey (2024), 78% of councils..."
   - AI is a tool to help you find sources, not a source itself

3. **Ask AI to explain its reasoning, not just conclusions**
   - Bad: "Is FreedomCamp-Manager a good idea?"
   - Good: "What are the top 5 reasons councils might adopt FreedomCamp-Manager? For each, what evidence supports this reason?"
   - Explanation gives you facts to verify

4. **Use AI to identify sources to check, not as a primary lookup tool**
   - Bad: "What does the Freedom Camping Act say about fines?"
   - Good: "What sections of the Freedom Camping Act cover council enforcement powers?" Then: Read the Act yourself at legislation.govt.nz
   - AI helps you know WHAT to look for; primary source tells you WHERE to find it

5. **Ask AI to label confidence levels**
   - Good: "I'm researching council enforcement demand. What sources would you recommend, and how confident are you in each?"
   - AI response: "High confidence: LGNZ survey (2024), peer-reviewed research. Medium confidence: News articles. Low confidence: Anecdotes or vendor claims."

### Red Flags for AI Lies (Hallucinations)

When AI tells you something, check for these warning signs:

1. **Vague citations**: "Studies show..." but no link (BIG red flag for AI)
2. **Too-specific numbers**: "78.4% of councils" (oddly precise without methodology)
3. **Quoted text you can't find**: AI gives a quote but you can't find it anywhere
4. **Circular reasoning**: "Source A says what I read in Source B" but URL doesn't match
5. **Recent data**: "As of 2026" but the resource was last updated 2015
6. **Confident about unknowns**: "Councils will definitely do X" when the future is uncertain

**Best practice**: When AI provides a factual claim:
1. Check the cited source directly
2. Verify AI's quote against original
3. If it matches: Use it; if it differs: Reject it

## Bulletin 8: Building Your Own Research Framework

**Type:** system  
**Title:** Research Protocol for FreedomCamp-Manager Market & Technical Analysis  
**Effective Date:** 2026-04-22

### Research Questions Worth Asking (vs. Quick Guesses)

**Good research question** (verifiable, answerable):
> "What percentage of NZ councils have adopted digital patrol management tools, and what barriers exist?"

**Bad research question** (vague, opinion-based):
> "Do councils like technology?"

**Good question**: Specific, measurable, grounded in real evidence  
**Bad question**: Assumes an answer, can't be verified

### Research Protocol for Market Analysis

When you investigate market opportunity, follow this steps:

1. **Define the question precisely**
   - Bad: "Is there a market for FreedomCamp-Manager?"
   - Good: "What percentage of NZ councils currently use digital tools for freedom camping enforcement, and what capabilities are they seeking?"

2. **Identify primary sources**
   - LGNZ survey data (2024)
   - Council Long-Term Plans (public documents)
   - Council procurement records (GETS.govt.nz)
   - Vendor comparison (existing tools analysis)
   - Freedom Camping Act 2011 (council powers)

3. **Verify with 2+ independent sources**
   - Don't stop at one council's LTP
   - Check LGNZ aggregate data
   - Review news coverage of council enforcement
   - Check what tools are already in use (council websites, procurement records)

4. **Note data gaps**
   - "No published data on council digital adoption rate—used proxy of infrastructure spending"
   - "Survey has 45 respondents out of 67 councils—may overrepresent tech-aware councils"

5. **Separate fact from interpretation**
   - Fact: "LGNZ survey shows 78% of councils prioritize enforcement"
   - Interpretation: "This suggests FreedomCamp-Manager adoption potential is high (interpreted as 70%+) because enforcement priority correlates with tool adoption"

6. **Publish your reasoning**
   - Tell the user: sources, assumptions, limitations, confidence level
   - Example above shows good model

### Research Protocol for Technical Analysis

When analyzing technology claims:

1. **Find the official source**
   - Claim: "Ollama supports GPT-4o"
   - Check: Ollama GitHub / official docs
   - Result: FALSE (Ollama supports open models like Llama; doesn't support proprietary OpenAI models)

2. **Test the claim if possible**
   - For "Model X runs on infrastructure Y"
   - Try it, see if it works, document what happens

3. **Check version and date**
   - "Feature X was added in v2.5" → Verify current version
   - "This doc is from 2022" → Likely outdated for fast-moving tech

4. **Identify breaking changes**
   - If library updated, did it break the claim?
   - Always check library `CHANGELOG.md` for breaking changes

5. **Document your environment**
   - "Tested on: Ollama 0.1.32, llama3.1:8b model, RunPod container"
   - "This may differ on other hardware/versions"
