# Bob NZ Councils & Procurement Growth Training Pack

## Purpose

Train Bob on the specific NZ council, regulatory, and public-sector procurement context essential for FreedomCamp-Manager's growth into council and regional authority adoption.

## Council Operational Landscape

### Freedom Camping Act 2011 and Bylaw Framework

Bob should understand:

- The Freedom Camping Act 2011 is the primary NZ statute for freedom camping regulation
- Councils enforce via local bylaws registered under the Act
- Bylaws can prohibit freedom camping in specific areas or apply permit/fee requirements
- Enforcement includes warnings, infringement notices, and removal of vehicles
- Breach penalties: up to NZ$20,000 fine + removal costs passed to owner

Key documentation:
- Legislation.govt.nz — Freedom Camping Act 2011 full text
- Department of Internal Affairs — Policy guidance on Act implementation
- Individual council bylaw websites for specific regional rules and maps

### Councils' Operational Needs Around Freedom Camping & Public Land Patrol

Bob should reason about:

1. **Breach Visibility**: Council wants live patrol + incident reporting so ratepayers see enforcement activity
2. **Evidence Chain**: Infringement notices must be defensible; photo, GPS, and timestamped evidence is mandatory
3. **Stakeholder Reporting**: Regional councils, district councils, and iwi partnerships require SLA-level transparency
4. **Cost Recovery**: Many councils expect to recover enforcement costs from fines or pass to venue operators (holiday parks, campgrounds)
5. **Reputational Risk**: Poor enforcement or inconsistent application invites legal challenge and public criticism
6. **Operational Efficiency**: Patrol routes should optimize area and response time; analytics inform resource allocation

### Council Procurement Patterns & Buyer Behavior

NZ council procurement follows MBIE Procurement Rules and local governance codes.

Key patterns:

1. **Budget Constraints**: Councils operate fixed annual budgets; capex for software is often restricted to strategic capex funding. Operational expenditure on software subscriptions is easier to approve than large one-time license fees.
2. **RFI/RFP Cycles**: Multi-million-dollar contracts are tendered; smaller contracts ($200K–$1M) may be single-supplier negotiated if the supplier is proven
3. **Broader Outcomes Requirement**: Councils must address social value, employment outcomes, Maori engagement, and environmental sustainability in procurement
4. **Local Government Reform Context** (2024–2026): Three waters reforms, amalgamations, and service consolidations create churn and risk aversion; stability and vendor viability matter
5. **Single-Supplier/Lock-In Risk**: Councils fear becoming dependent on proprietary software and data export restrictions; they prefer open APIs and vendor-neutral data formats
6. **Procurement Decision Chain**: Tech decisions are typically made by infrastructure or operations teams, but budget approval requires senior leadership (CFO, chief executive). The RFP/procurement sponsor is often a different role than the end-user.

## Regional Authority & Special-Purpose Vehicle Context

Bob should understand:

- **Auckland Council** (supercity, ~1.6M residents): Large, hierarchical IT procurement; tends toward enterprise vendors. Freedom camping enforcement is less prominent than waste, transport, and development.
- **Territorial Authorities** (13 district councils + Unitary Authorities): More agile procurement; willing to trial smaller vendors. Regional enforcement varies by geography; coastal regions (Tasman, Southland, Otago) have higher freedom camping pressure.
- **Iwi Authorities** (Maori governance bodies): Increasingly co-manage public land and heritage sites. They contract out enforcement but expect cultural sensitivity, community benefit, and data sovereignty in vendor arrangements.
- **Regional Tourism & Business Associations**: Sometimes coordinate enforcement on behalf of local councils or destination management organizations (e.g., Nelson/Tasman tourism board).

## Council Adoption Success Factors

Bob should reason about these adoption drivers:

- **Pilot or Sandbox Approach**: Councils often trial software on one area (e.g., single parking zone, one patrol route) before rollout
- **Multi-Council Bulk Buying**: Some procurement occurs through regional or sector-wide buying panels (e.g., LGAP — Local Government Association Panel) to reduce cost per council
- **Continuous Compliance**: The software must support audit, retention, and public-information requests (Official Information Act 1982)
- **Staff Turnover & Training**: Councils have high operational-staff turnover; vendor must support onboarding and training without frequent consulting engagements
- **Data Sovereignty & Security**: Councils are increasingly aware of data security obligations; they expect NZ-hosted infrastructure, regular backups, and incident response SLAs
- **Asset Lifecycle & Tech Refresh**: Council IT budgets cycle with asset-refresh timelines; upgrades and new software often align with multi-year IT roadmaps

## Procurement Language & Tender Response Guidance

When Bob helps draft tender responses for councils, emphasize:

1. **Broader Outcomes**: Explicitly map vendor capability to council's stated social value goals (e.g., Maori employment in enforcement, trainee officer pathways, regional development)
2. **Sustainability & Cloud**: Confirm NZ cloud hosting, energy efficiency, and data sovereignty compliance
3. **Service Model Clarity**: Define whether the vendor provides software, managed service, or both; cost structure (per-seat, per-patrol, per-case) and what is included in each tier
4. **Support & SLA**: Specify availability (e.g., 99% uptime SLA), incident response times (e.g., critical < 2 hours), and escalation path
5. **Training & Transition**: Outline onboarding timeline, staff training duration, data migration process, and go-live support
6. **Exit & Data Portability**: Address what happens if the contract ends (e.g., data export in open format, 30-day standdown, no lock-in fees)
7. **Auditing & Compliance**: Confirm the software supports OIA requests, audit trails, retention policies, and third-party certification (e.g., ISO 27001 if applicable)

## Recommended Council Adoption Sequence

Bob should advise this geographic and scale-based approach:

**Phase 1 (Proof of Concept)**: Target 1–2 smaller district councils or unitary authorities with:
- Clear freedom camping problem (coastal region, holiday park concentration)
- Existing enforcement team (5–15 officers)
- Executive sponsor who is receptive to technology innovation
- Budget for pilot (NZD $50K–$150K over 12 months)

Example regions: Tasman District, Nelson City, Southland District, West Coast District, Otago (Clutha/Catlins area).

**Phase 2 (Regional Anchor)**: Expand to a mid-size territorial authority (population 50K–150K) such as:
- Rotorua
- Wanganui
- Gisborne

**Phase 3 (Metro Trails)**: Once product is proven and case studies exist, approach larger councils through:
- Regional buying panels or LGAP
- Strategic partnership with regional tourism bodies
- Direct outreach to councils undergoing procurement refresh cycles

**Phase 4 (National Scale)**: Auckland + Wellington adoption, if the product can handle large, complex governance and high-volume enforcement workloads.

## NZ Public-Sector Digital & Policy Context

Bob should stay aware of:

- **Government Digital Service (GDS) Standards** (https://www.digital.govt.nz/standards/) — design, accessibility, and security principles expected in public-sector digital services
- **Privacy Act 2020** — enforcement data must comply; personal data (officer identity, complainant contact, etc.) must be protected; subject access requests must be honored within 20 days
- **Public Records Act 2005** — enforcement case files may be part of council records; retention schedule and long-term preservation matter
- **Official Information Act 1982** — councils must publish some enforcement statistics and may face OIA requests for complaint logs, incident data, and decision rationales
